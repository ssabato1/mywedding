Weddings look different from country to country, but what the rituals do is remarkably similar: they bind two people, join two families and show a promise before a community. Here are wedding rituals collected by the overseas wedding press, grouped by what they do, and how an international couple can fit two cultures' rituals into one wedding. (As of December 2026)

## Binding: rituals that show two people have become one

In the wedding magazine THE WED's [roundup of wedding rituals](https://thewed.com/magazine/historical-cultural-symbolic-wedding-rituals), the action that appears most often is binding.

- **Celtic handfasting.** The couple's wrists are bound with cord or ribbon, making marriage a visible knot — connected with the English phrase "tying the knot".
- **El lazo, Mexico.** A figure-eight cord or rosary is draped over the couple's shoulders. THE WED traces the tradition to seventh-century Spain.
- **Cord and veil, the Philippines.** According to The Knot's [collection of wedding traditions around the world](https://www.theknot.com/content/favorite-wedding-traditions-from-around-the-world), a cord and veil are placed over the couple to show their oneness. Thirteen coins (arras) are also exchanged as a promise to provide for each other.
- **Stefana, Greek Orthodox.** Two crowns joined by a white ribbon are placed on the couple's heads, making them king and queen of their new household.

## Offering: rituals that join two families

- **The Chinese tea ceremony.** The couple serve tea to the elders of both families in order of seniority. THE WED describes it as reflecting Confucian filial piety, and The Knot adds that the elders respond with red envelopes.
- **Pyebaek, Korea.** The scene The Knot presents as Korean tradition is the bride catching chestnuts and dates thrown by the elders in her skirt. Like the tea ceremony, it is the moment the new family member greets the elders.
- **Jaimala, Hindu.** The couple place flower garlands around each other's necks as a sign of accepting each other. Before the wedding, a mehndi party is held where henna patterns are drawn on the hands of the bride and her family and friends.

Tea, pyebaek and garlands all sit before or after the vows, so they fit into one wedding without overlapping another culture's ceremony.

## Breaking, jumping, sawing: the first thing done together

- **Breaking the glass, Jewish.** The ceremony takes place under the chuppah (a canopy on four poles, a home without walls), and ends with a glass broken underfoot — remembering loss even in joy.
- **Polterabend and log sawing, Germany.** The night before, porcelain is smashed for luck and the couple clear it up together. On the day they saw through a log. Both show that they can work together.
- **Jumping the broom.** The couple jump over a broom at the end of the ceremony. The Knot presents it as a Black and African-American tradition.
- **The money spray, Nigeria.** At the reception guests shower the dancing couple with banknotes as a blessing.

## The history of what we take for granted

The parts that seem natural today have origins too. THE WED writes that **the father walking the bride down the aisle** was originally a legal transfer from father to husband and is now reinterpreted as a gesture of gratitude. **The veil** began as the flame-coloured veil of the Roman bride, meant to ward off evil spirits, and later became a symbol of purity and modesty. **The petals and confetti** thrown at the exit come from the ancient Roman custom of throwing grain to wish for abundance.

Tradition is not fixed; it keeps being rewritten. So a couple may choose and change it.

## Fitting two cultures' rituals into one wedding

1. **Choose one from each side.** Include everything and the ceremony runs past an hour. One ritual from each culture, the one that means most to the family, is enough.
2. **Choose rituals of different kinds.** One that binds and one that offers, so the actions do not repeat.
3. **Explain the meaning.** One sentence from the MC, or two languages in the programme. A ritual nobody understands ends as a spectacle.
4. **Divide the places.** Vows and rings in the ceremony; tea or pyebaek with the family before or after; the money spray or dancing at the reception.
5. **Check the props with the venue.** A tea table, a glass, cord, candles — some items need agreement to bring in and use.

## What to check when applying this in Korea

| What to check | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Whether the couple can set the running order | A ritual needs a place in the order |
| Whether there is enough time | Each ritual needs five to ten minutes |
| Whether there is a pyebaek room or a separate family space | Tea and pyebaek need a quiet room |
| Whether candles, glass and petals may be used | Flame and breakage need prior agreement |
| Whether two languages are possible | Both families need to know what the ritual means |

For example, at Sevit Island and Marina Park on the Han River in Seoul the couple sets the running order, English-language coordination and a pyebaek room are available, and within a generous time slot of three hours or more the two cultures' rituals can be divided between ceremony and reception. At any venue, ask first whether the time and props for the ritual you want are possible.
