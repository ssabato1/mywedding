What remains in a guest's hand after the wedding is the favour. It is a small gift, but it is the only part of the wedding that follows the guest home. Why are favours given, and why sugared almonds of all things? Here are the origins, the customs of different countries, and what to consider when choosing a favour today.

## The beginning: an aristocrat's box of sweets

According to the British wedding site Guides for Brides, in its [history of wedding favours](https://www.guidesforbrides.co.uk/blog/the-history-of-wedding-favours), the favour began centuries ago among European aristocrats, who gave their guests a small box called a bonbonnière. Made of crystal, porcelain, gold or silver, it held sweets.

More precious than the box was the sugar inside. Sugar was costly, a symbol of wealth and royalty. A wedding was considered a lucky occasion, and the gift was a way of sharing that luck with the guests.

## The meaning of five almonds

Sugar was not within everyone's reach. Families of more modest means gave love knots of ribbon and lace, or embroidered linen bags of almonds. A bag usually held five almonds, standing for five wishes for the couple.

| Almond | Wish |
| --- | --- |
| First | Health |
| Second | Wealth |
| Third | Happiness |
| Fourth | Fertility |
| Fifth | Longevity |

Around the thirteenth century sugar became more affordable and almonds began to be coated in it. These were called confetti. They later developed into the coloured, hard-shelled sweets still familiar today, and sugared almonds wrapped in fabric and tied with ribbon became the standard wedding favour.

## Bitter and sweet: the Jordan almond

In the English-speaking world the sweet is called the Jordan almond. The American wedding publication The Knot explains its meaning in its [guide to Jordan almonds](https://www.theknot.com/content/all-about-jordan-almonds): the bitterness of the almond stands for the hardships of life, and the sugar coating for the hope that life together will be sweeter. Life is both bitter and sweet.

The origin of the name is uncertain. Some trace it to the French word for garden, others to almonds grown near the River Jordan. Written records go back to about 1350, and the ancient Romans are said to have used them at celebrations. The earliest versions were probably coated in honey rather than sugar.

## Favours in different countries

| Country | Favour | Custom |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Italy | Bomboniere | Five sugared almonds in a small box or tulle bag, marked with the couple's names and the date |
| Greece | Koufeta | Served on a silver tray, always in odd numbers. An odd number cannot be divided, so the couple will not be parted |
| English-speaking world | Jordan almonds, small gifts | Placed at each setting or handed out as guests leave |
| Korea | Rice cakes and household goods; now many kinds of gift | Often given in place of the meal to guests who leave without dining |

Colour carries meaning too. According to The Knot, white almonds are common at weddings, green at engagement parties and red at graduations, though meanings vary by tradition.

The Korean favour serves a slightly different purpose. Abroad the favour is a keepsake for every guest; in Korea it is often a gift in place of the meal. At an international wedding it is worth preparing the two separately.

## Favours today

Favours now go far beyond sugared almonds. They fall into three kinds.

- **Things to eat**: biscuits, chocolate, tea, honey, jam, olive oil. They place no burden on the guest and are not thrown away.
- **Things to use**: candles, soap, matches, a small potted plant.
- **Things with meaning**: a donation in the couple's name announced on a card, or a speciality of the couple's home towns.

At an international wedding the favour can introduce a culture: traditional Korean sweets or tea for the overseas guests, and sweets from the partner's country for the Korean guests.

## How to choose a favour

**Choose something to eat or to use.** An ornament engraved with names and a date tends to end up in a drawer. Guests remember what they actually use.

**It must be easy to carry.** Avoid anything heavy, fragile or bulky. Overseas guests fly home, and liquids and fresh food can be stopped at security or customs.

**Think of the season.** Chocolate and fresh flowers will not last a summer wedding. For an outdoor wedding, choose something that withstands sun and heat.

**Match the wrapping to the design of the wedding.** The favour sits on the table with the napkin, the menu card and the flowers. Matching the ribbon and tag to the invitation makes them one set. Paper design is covered in [Wedding Invitations and Stationery](/blog/wedding-invitations-and-stationery-guide-en).

**Check for allergies.** Nuts are the tradition, but they can be dangerous for a guest with an allergy. Write the ingredients on the wrapping.

**Count by household.** A couple or family attending together can share one. If the favour replaces the meal, though, prepare one per person.

## Where to put them and when to give them

- **At each place.** On the plate or beside the napkin, the favour becomes part of the table. This suits assigned seating.
- **On a table at the entrance.** Guests take one as they leave. A sign is needed, or they will walk past.
- **From your own hands.** Giving one to each guest as you see them off is the most gracious way, and the slowest.

## What to check when applying this in Korea

| What to check | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Favours in place of the meal | Estimate the number of guests who will not dine, and settle the guaranteed number and the favour count together |
| Outside food | Ask whether edible favours may be brought into the venue and where they can be stored |
| Handing them out | Decide whether they are given at the reception desk, placed at the seats or handed out at the exit, and who is responsible |
| Leftovers | Decide in advance who takes home what remains |
| Overseas guests | Check that the item can be carried on a plane and brought into the guest's country |
| Labelling | For food, write the ingredients and the use-by date on the wrapping |

From the aristocrat's sugar box to five almonds to today's small gift, the form has changed. The meaning is what it was at the start: sharing the luck of the day with those who came.
