In the work of five florists featured by the overseas wedding press since 2025, what carries over to a wedding in Korea is less a list of flowers than a way of reading the place and designing form. (As of November 2026)

![An aisle filled with flowers and candles — concept image](/images/floral-aisle-concept-01.jpg)

## 1. Gabriela Salazar: a scene that seems to have grown there

At [a Blackberry Farm wedding featured in Vogue](https://www.vogue.com/slideshow/serena-marron-and-nick-rugoff-wedding), Gabriela Salazar used the vegetation and moss of the surrounding hills so that the aisle flowers looked as if they had grown on the spot. Repeating the pink and orange of the sunset in flowers and fabric was central too. Her work is at [@lamusadelasflores](https://www.instagram.com/lamusadelasflores/).

In Korea, rather than gathering wild flowers, first check the site manager's permission and what is in season locally. Set out low drifts of flowers and hide the base of structures with moss or leaves, and it looks like a garden that was always there without using many flowers.

## 2. Ren MacDonald-Balasia: locality and painterly scale

At [a wedding in Hawaii featured in Vogue](https://www.vogue.com/slideshow/alexa-dell-and-frederic-choquette-wedding), Ren MacDonald-Balasia drew on local materials and traditional techniques and composed orchids and alliums as if they had sprung up naturally. At [a wedding in Los Angeles](https://www.vogue.com/slideshow/ariel-rezek-and-lindsay-apatow-wedding) she bound lavish, oil-painting-like flowers, vivid colour and a butterfly motif into one landscape. Her account is [@renkofloral](https://www.instagram.com/renkofloral/).

To recreate this, build on local seasonal branches and foliage and use rare imported flowers only at the focal point for photographs. A couple's symbol sits more naturally woven into colour and repeated pattern than attached as a separate decoration.

![The power of one colour — a floral arch built from white blooms alone (concept image)](/images/floral-arch-concept-01.jpg)

## 3. Rachel Cho: asymmetry and the power of one variety

In [Brides' 2025 flower trends](https://www.brides.com/wedding-flower-trends-2025-8755099), Rachel Cho explains that an asymmetrical arch creates movement and air, and that even a single variety makes a strong scene when arranged loosely and organically. Her account is [@rachelchofloral](https://www.instagram.com/rachelchofloral/).

In Korea, choose one flower available in steady quantity that season and vary its height and direction. A frame weighted to one side or open in its curve, rather than a perfect circle, brings the silhouette alive without adding varieties.

## 4. Maddie Haley: living sculpture

In its [2025 Ones to Watch](https://www.theknot.com/content/ones-to-watch), The Knot introduces Maddie Haley's Absūna as "living sculpture" that starts from irregular growth and movement, and notes her preference for reusable structures and locally grown flowers over floral foam. Her account is [@absuna__](https://www.instagram.com/absuna__/).

Instead of a mood board listing flower names, set a vocabulary of form: a line flowing to the left, deliberate empty space, irregular heights. At the quotation stage, ask about seasonal flowers from Korean growers, reusable frames and whether floral foam is used.

![Living sculpture — a floral arch of hydrangea and delphinium (a wedding venue on the Han River in Seoul)](/images/sevit-garden-02.jpg)

## 5. Naomi Azriel: a still life made from feeling

The same [article in The Knot](https://www.theknot.com/content/ones-to-watch) explains that Naomi Azriel's Nectar & Vessel begins a design from the feeling the couple wants and composes flowers and produce like a Dutch still life. Small variations from table to table and sourcing most materials from local farms and women-owned businesses are also characteristic. Her account is [@nectarandvessel](https://www.instagram.com/nectarandvessel/).

In Korea, choose one word first — abundance, calm or festivity — then mix flowers and fruit of the same season low on the table. Keep the colour consistent but vary the vessels and heights a little from table to table, and it feels less like duplicated decoration and more like walking through an exhibition.

## What to check when applying this in Korea

| Item | Guideline |
| --- | --- |
| Seasonal supply | Build on seasonal Korean flowers rather than rare imports, and agree substitutes |
| Structure and safety | Check the load of arches and ceiling pieces, evacuation routes and the rules on candles |
| Sustainability | Ask about floral foam, reusable frames and where the flowers go afterwards |
| Proportion | Do not rely on photographs; scale to the aisle width and ceiling height |
| Time | In a large space such as Sevit Island, plan installation, changeover and removal separately from the running order |

![Orchids, roses and hydrangeas layered in the arch, in detail (concept image)](/images/floral-arch-concept-02.jpg)

## Summary

What the five have in common is not using many expensive flowers. They read the colours and vegetation of the place, settle on one principle of form and adjust the scale with seasonal materials. When recreating their work in Korea, rather than copying a piece, hold clearly to one principle: ground-level drifts, asymmetrical line, a single variety, reusable structure or still-life tables.
