One of the scenes most often seen in wedding films from abroad is the first dance: the bride and groom dancing alone while the guests stand around them. It was not part of the Korean wedding, but Korea has a custom that has long filled the same place, the congratulatory song. Here is where each comes from, how they differ, and how to include both in one wedding. Entrance music is covered in [Choosing the Bride's Entrance Music](/blog/bridal-entrance-music-en) and [Choosing the Parents' Entrance Music](/blog/parents-entrance-music-en).

## Where the first dance comes from: opening the ball

According to Wikipedia's entry on the [first dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_dance), it belonged originally to the ball, not the wedding. At a formal ball the guest of honour, the person of highest rank present, led the opening dance. Because these were long dances performed in a line, the guests of honour were simply the first couple down the set.

The dance that opened the ball changed with the times.

| Time and place | Opening dance |
| --- | --- |
| Seventeenth-century France | The minuet, called the "Queen of Dances" |
| Victorian Britain | The quadrille |
| Nineteenth-century Russian Empire | The polonaise |

At a wedding reception the newlyweds are the guests of honour, so they open the dancing. "To open the ball" survives as a figure of speech for starting something others will continue.

## The first dance as a performance

The form we know, in which the couple dances alone while everyone watches, is not very old. Wikipedia records the advice of the American etiquette writer Emily Post in 1922: the newlyweds should wait until the guests had begun to dance, then dance with each other before taking other partners. The first dance was not then a performance.

Today couples choose their own way. Some choreograph and rehearse a routine, some simply sway, some learn a ballroom style. The music is often a hit of the moment. Some, like the etiquette writer Judith Martin, consider a choreographed duet for spectators inappropriate. There is no fixed answer.

## The order of dances at a reception abroad

At receptions in the English-speaking world, dancing usually begins after the meal. A common order:

1. **The first dance**: the bride and groom alone.
2. **The parent dances**: the bride with her father, the groom with his mother, as thanks to those who raised them.
3. **Everyone**: the host or band invites the guests and the dance floor opens.

Some couples place the first dance before the meal, straight after their entrance to the reception, when every eye is already on them. The flow of the whole reception is set out in [Wedding Party Culture: From Cocktail Hour to After-Party](/blog/wedding-party-culture-cocktail-hour-to-after-party-en).

## The Korean wedding song

What has held the place of the first dance at a Korean wedding is the congratulatory song. During the ceremony a friend, a relative or sometimes the groom himself sings. If the dance is the moment the couple stands before the guests, the song is the moment a guest stands before the couple. The direction is reversed, but both create the point of highest feeling in the wedding.

What to consider when preparing a song:

- **Length**: one song of three to four minutes. More than two songs makes the ceremony drag.
- **Accompaniment**: decide whether to use a backing track or live piano or strings, and agree the key and tempo with the sound technician.
- **Position**: the singer faces the couple. Facing the guests turns it into a concert.
- **Rehearsal**: check the microphone level and the cue for the accompaniment once before the ceremony.

## Putting both into one wedding

An international wedding, or one that includes a reception, can have both the song and the first dance. The key is to separate them.

| Where | What | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The ceremony | The song | It is where Korean guests expect it, and it keeps the formality of the ceremony |
| After the meal or at the after-party | First dance and parent dances | The mood has loosened, and guests follow naturally onto the floor |

If dancing feels daunting, the form can be reduced.

- **Dance for ninety seconds.** Rather than the whole song, have the host invite the guests to join partway through.
- **Dance without choreography.** Holding each other and turning slowly is enough.
- **Dance to live music.** Strings or piano are slow enough to dance at a walking pace.
- **Make the song the dance.** If a friend sings while the two of you dance, the two moments become one.

## Choosing the music

- **Your own song** comes first: the song you first heard together, or the one from the proposal.
- **Check the tempo.** A song at a slow walking pace works both for dancing and for singing.
- **Read the lyrics to the end.** Many songs with sweet melodies are about parting.
- **Shorten it.** If the original runs over four minutes, prepare an edit.
- **Think of the ages of your guests.** A song known to both parents and friends is well received.

## What to check when applying this in Korea

| What to check | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Space to dance | Ask the venue whether there is room between the tables, or whether the aisle or the area before the stage can be used |
| Length of the wedding | Including both dance and song adds time. Work out from the order of ceremony whether it fits the venue's time slot |
| Sound and lighting | Agree the file format, the number of microphones and the lighting for the dance with the technician |
| Musicians and singers | Check whether outside performers are allowed and whether there is time to rehearse |
| Use of music | If you plan to post the film online, check how the music may be used |
| Explaining it to guests | For guests unfamiliar with the dance, have the host introduce it in a sentence |

Whether it is a dance or a song, the meaning is the same: a few minutes in the middle of the wedding that belong only to the two of you. Choose the form that feels natural to you.
